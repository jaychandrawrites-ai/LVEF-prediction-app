What is moving this result

Research prototype — not a medical device. This tool has not been clinically validated or reviewed by any regulator. It cannot diagnose anyone and must not be used to decide whether a person receives care. If you have symptoms such as breathlessness, chest pain or swelling, contact a clinician.

How to read the result

The tool answers one question: how likely is it that an echocardiogram would find an ejection fraction of 45% or below. It ranks patients by that risk. It does not measure ejection fraction, and the number it estimates carries an interval roughly 24 points wide in each direction — wide enough that the point estimate should never be treated as a measurement.

The screening threshold is set for 90% sensitivity. At that setting, in a population resembling the test set, the tool clears about 35% of patients from needing imaging while missing about 10% of those who genuinely have a reduced ejection fraction. A result below the threshold lowers the probability of disease; it does not exclude it.

Accuracy is not uniform. The model draws most of its signal from abnormal conduction. Among patients with a QRS under about 88 ms it explains essentially none of the variation in ejection fraction, because a narrow QRS is compatible with almost any contractile state. Among patients with wider complexes it performs considerably better. This mirrors the long-standing clinical finding that a prolonged QRS is specific for left ventricular dysfunction but poorly sensitive.

Where the numbers come from

A neural network with two hidden layers of 64 units, trained on 65,928 electrocardiograms from the EchoNext database and evaluated on 4,822 recordings from patients who never appeared in training. The database splits by patient rather than by recording, so no individual contributes to both sides.
